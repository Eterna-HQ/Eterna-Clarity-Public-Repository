# If I'm Unavailable: Household Information Plan

Use this as a **starting map**, not a place to copy every household record. Write enough that someone you trust can understand what needs attention and where the real information lives if you cannot answer questions for a while.

**Do not put passwords, PINs, recovery codes, encryption keys, banking credentials, security answers or other secrets in this plan.**

Even without passwords, this plan can reveal private household information. Keep it only where appropriate and share it only with people who should have it.

Listing someone here does **not** give them legal permission to act for you. Point to the document, institution or formal arrangement that actually provides authority where one is required.

## 7. Test whether another person can use the plan

Imagine you cannot answer follow-up questions.

- [ ] They know who to contact first.
- [ ] They can find the records and systems that actually matter.
- [ ] They can see current situations that should not be forgotten.
- [ ] They can identify recurring responsibilities that cannot be missed.
- [ ] They know where protected access or recovery arrangements are managed without needing secrets written here.
- [ ] They can tell which named people are contacts and which authority comes from a formal document or account arrangement.

If the test fails, improve the path to the information. Do not solve every gap by copying more sensitive information into this plan.

## 8. Review when life changes

Review this plan after a move, relationship change, birth or death, new dependant, major account or property change, new or changed legal document, health or care change, insurance change, new caregiver, major provider change, or change in who you trust to be part of the handover.

A quick periodic review is also useful because contact information, responsibilities and access arrangements can quietly become stale.
